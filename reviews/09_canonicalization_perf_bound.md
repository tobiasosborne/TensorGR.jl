# Speed-of-Light Bound for the Proposed Canonicalization Core

**Date**: 2026-09-29 · **Author**: Claude (Opus 5.5) · **Companion to**: `08_canonicalization_literature_survey.md` §10

**Question.** For the proposed core — encode a tensor monomial as a coloured graph, canonicalize by
individualization–refinement (IR), collect terms by hashing — what is the best achievable time per
term on a generic desktop CPU and a basic discrete GPU, given clock speed, cache, memory bandwidth
and latency?

**Status of the numbers.** Two kinds of statement appear below. *Floors* in §3 are genuine lower bounds
for any algorithm. Everything from §4 on is a **model lower bound for this algorithm**: it counts the
minimum plausible work of an ideal IR implementation (perfect pruning, minimal instruction count per
primitive) and divides by the hardware's peak sustainable rates. Real implementations can only be slower.
The model script is `09_probes/bound.py`; every table is generated by it.

## 0. Summary

| Case | 1 core, ideal | 1 core, practical | 8 cores, practical, per term | GPU offload floor, per term |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A: distinct factors, $n=12$ | 43 ns | 83 ns | 10.4 ns | 9.8 ns |
| B: $\mathrm{Riem}^3$ invariant, $n=12$ | 144 ns | 280 ns | 35.0 ns | 9.8 ns |
| C: quadratic-action term, $n=24$ | 167 ns | 327 ns | 40.9 ns | 17.2 ns |
| D: $\mathrm{Riem}^8$ chain, $n=32$ | 889 ns | 1620 ns | 202.5 ns | 35.3 ns |

1. **The CPU core is compute-bound, not memory-bound.** The per-instance working set is about $80n$ bytes
   (≤ 3 KB), which fits in L1 many times over. The memory floor for streaming terms and inserting into
   the collection hash table is 4–6 ns per term on 8 cores, below the compute floor in every case —
   *provided* hash inserts are batched so that about 16 cache misses per core are in flight. A naive
   dependent-probe insert costs one full DRAM latency (80 ns) per term per core, which would double
   case A.
2. **Branch mispredictions are about half of the practical floor.** The "ideal" column assumes
   branch-free refinement; the "practical" column charges 0.5 mispredicts per cell split. Branch-free
   refinement (bitset colour classes for ≤ 128 slots) is the largest single lever.
3. **Symmetric products pay for search, and the cost grows as $n^2$.** For $k$ identical factors the
   ideal IR search costs $\Theta(k\,(V+E))$, i.e. $\Theta(n^2)$ when $k \propto n$. That matches the
   ≈ $n^2$ scaling SeQuant measured (survey §3). $\mathrm{Riem}^8$ costs 6.2× $\mathrm{Riem}^3$ in total work, 2.3× per slot.
4. **A basic GPU only wins if terms never cross PCIe.** On-device floors are 0.7–35 ns per term, but
   the PCIe 4.0 ×8 transfer floor alone is 10–22 ns per term — comparable to the 8-core CPU for
   low-search terms. Offloading canonicalization alone buys at most 2–4× on B and C and nothing on A.
   The GPU case only becomes compelling when term generation, canonicalization and collection all stay
   on the device. For search-heavy terms the answer hinges on the unmeasured SIMT efficiency $\eta$.
5. **Current TensorGR is roughly 500–1000× above the practical floor.** Measured: 438 µs per
   $\mathrm{Riem}^3$ canonicalization and 1.64 ms per $\mathrm{Riem}^4$ on this laptop (§9).

$$
R_{\mathrm{int}} = N_{\mathrm{SM}} \cdot 64 \cdot f_{\mathrm{GPU}} = 24 \cdot 64 \cdot 2.46\times 10^{9} \approx 3.78\times 10^{12}\ \text{int ops/s}

## 2. Instance model and graph encoding

A tensor monomial has $m$ factors, $n$ index slots, $d$ dummy pairs and $f$ free indices, so
$n = 2d + f$. Let $k$ be the size of the largest set of identical, mutually exchangeable factors.

The encoding follows SeQuant (survey §3): one vertex per tensor core, per slot, and per index (a dummy
pair is one index vertex joined to two slots), plus $g$ "bundle" gadget vertices encoding slot
symmetries (a Riemann tensor uses 3: two antisymmetric pairs and a pair-exchange bundle). Every slot has
one edge to its index and one edge to its bundle or core, and every bundle has one edge to its parent:

$$
V = m + n + d + f + g, \qquad E = 2n + g .
$$

The per-instance working set is CSR adjacency plus about four partition arrays, roughly
$4(V+1) + 8E + 16V \approx 80n$ bytes: 1–3 KB for the cases below, far inside L1.

| Case | $m$ | $n$ | $d$ | $f$ | $g$ | $k$ | $V$ | $E$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A: distinct factors | 3 | 12 | 6 | 0 | 3 | 1 | 24 | 27 |
| B: $R_{ab}{}^{cd} R_{cd}{}^{ef} R_{ef}{}^{ab}$ | 3 | 12 | 6 | 0 | 9 | 3 | 30 | 33 |
| C: quadratic-action term ($h\,\nabla\nabla h \times$ background) | 4 | 24 | 11 | 2 | 6 | 2 | 47 | 54 |
| D: $\mathrm{Riem}^8$ chain | 8 | 32 | 16 | 0 | 24 | 8 | 80 | 88 |

## 3. Algorithm-independent floors

Any canonicalizer must read the input and write the output. With a packed encoding of 4 bytes per slot
plus a 16-byte header, $b_{\mathrm{term}} = 4n + 16$ bytes. On one core this costs at least
$n/(\iota f)$ — under 3 ns here, negligible. At scale, streaming terms in and out of DRAM costs

$$
\tau_{\mathrm{stream}} \ge \frac{2\,b_{\mathrm{term}}}{B} ,
$$

which is 1.8–4.1 ns per term for $n = 12$–$32$. This is the only rigorous floor; everything after it is
specific to the IR algorithm.

---

## 4. Work model for ideal IR

Counts are in µops — the minimum plausible for each primitive.

**Graph construction.** Decode the input and build CSR adjacency:

$$
W_{\mathrm{build}} = 4n + 2E .
$$

**One full colour refinement.** Each of the $2E$ adjacency entries must be touched at least once:
load the neighbour, load its colour, increment a count, compare — about 4 µops. Re-bucketing each
vertex costs about 6 µops:

$$
W_{\mathrm{ref}} = 4 \cdot 2E + 6V = 8E + 6V .
$$

This is a *lower* bound per refinement: Hopcroft-style refinement is $O((V+E)\log V)$ in the worst case,
and nauty/Traces also maintain invariant hashes.

**Leaf and output.** Relabelling by the discrete partition and forming or comparing the certificate
costs $W_{\mathrm{leaf}} = 3(V+E)$. Emitting canonical indices and the sign costs $W_{\mathrm{out}} = 2n$.

**Search for $k$ identical factors.** The factors are permuted by $S_k$.
- The first path individualizes $k-1$ factors (the last is forced), i.e. $k-1$ refinements.
- To prove the canonical choice, the search must discover generators of $S_k$. With ideal orbit
  pruning, working upward from the deepest level, depth $i$ needs one sibling explored down to a leaf,
  $k-i$ individualizations, yielding the adjacent transposition $(i,\,i+1)$. Those $k-1$ Coxeter
  generators generate $S_k$ and complete every orbit.

The number of individualizations is therefore

$$
N_{\mathrm{ind}} = (k-1) + \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} (k-i) = \frac{(k-1)(k+2)}{2} .
$$

An incremental refinement after individualizing one factor touches roughly that factor's share of the
graph, $W_{\mathrm{ref}}/k$. Each discovered generator also needs a leaf and a verification pass of
$2(V+E)$ — mandatory if certificates are emitted — and a sign-character evaluation of $n$ µops. So

$$
W_{\mathrm{search}} = N_{\mathrm{ind}} \frac{W_{\mathrm{ref}}}{k} + (k-1)\bigl(W_{\mathrm{leaf}} + 2(V+E)\bigr) + (k-1)\,n .
$$

**Total and scaling:**

$$
W = W_{\mathrm{build}} + W_{\mathrm{ref}} + W_{\mathrm{leaf}} + W_{\mathrm{out}} + W_{\mathrm{search}} .
$$

Since $N_{\mathrm{ind}}/k \approx (k+2)/2$, $W_{\mathrm{search}} = \Theta\bigl(k\,(V+E)\bigr)$. For products
of Riemann tensors $n = 4k$ and $V, E = \Theta(n)$, so $W = \Theta(n^2)$.

## 5. Single-core latency bound

Three limits apply: µop throughput, the dependent-load critical path, and branch mispredictions.
Refinement proceeds in rounds, and each round's edge scans are independent, so out-of-order execution
overlaps them. The critical path is therefore about 3 rounds per individualization, each a chain of 4
dependent L1 loads:

$$
C_{\mathrm{lat}} = 3\,(1 + N_{\mathrm{ind}}) \cdot 4\,\ell_1 .
$$

With $S = V + N_{\mathrm{ind}} V / k$ cell splits:

$$
t_1^{\mathrm{ideal}} = \frac{\max\bigl(W/\iota,\ C_{\mathrm{lat}}\bigr)}{f},
\qquad
t_1^{\mathrm{prac}} = \frac{\max\bigl(W/\iota + p\,b\,S,\ C_{\mathrm{lat}}\bigr)}{f}.
$$

The latency path is never binding here: for case D it is 2,160 cycles against 4,447 cycles of µop
throughput. Cache misses are absent because the working set is L1-resident.

---

## 6. Multi-core throughput bound (canonicalize and collect)

Terms are independent, so work parallelizes across terms (the parallel study in survey §12). Per term,
in aggregate over $P$ cores:

$$
\tau_{\mathrm{CPU}} = \max\left( \frac{t_1}{P},\ \frac{2\,b_{\mathrm{term}} + 2\cdot 64}{B},\ \frac{t_{\mathrm{mem}}}{P\,M} \right).
$$

- The second term streams terms and moves one 64-byte hash-table line in and out per insert.
- The third term is the latency floor for random hash-table inserts when the table exceeds L3 (about
  $10^7$ distinct terms), with $M$ misses in flight per core: $80/(8\cdot 16) = 0.63$ ns.
- Without batching or prefetching, a dependent insert costs $t_{\mathrm{mem}}/P = 10$ ns per term —
  as much as the entire canonicalization of case A.

---

## 7. GPU bound

**On-device floor.** Let $\eta$ be the SIMT efficiency: the fraction of lane-slots doing useful work
under warp divergence when a warp cooperatively refines one instance. Assume $\eta = 0.25$ for low-search
terms and $0.1$ for search-heavy ones; this is the key unmeasured parameter. Per term:

$$
\tau_{\mathrm{dev}} = \max\left( \frac{W}{\eta\,R_{\mathrm{int}}},\ \frac{2\,b_{\mathrm{term}} + 64}{B_{\mathrm{GPU}}},\ \frac{12\,(1+N_{\mathrm{ind}})\,\ell_{\mathrm{sh}}}{f_{\mathrm{GPU}}\,N_{\mathrm{conc}}} \right),
$$

with $N_{\mathrm{conc}} = N_{\mathrm{SM}} \cdot \min\bigl(48,\ \lfloor 100\,\mathrm{KB}/80n \rfloor\bigr)$
concurrent instances held in shared memory.

**Offload floor.** If terms are produced on the CPU they must cross PCIe both ways. With transfers fully
overlapped:

$$
\tau_{\mathrm{offload}} = \max\left( \tau_{\mathrm{dev}},\ \frac{2\,b_{\mathrm{term}}}{B_{\mathrm{PCIe}}} \right).
$$

## 9. Comparison with the current implementation (measured)

`canonicalize` on this laptop (`09_probes/time_xperm.jl`, 2,000 calls after warm-up):

| Instance | Measured | Allocated per call | ≈ at 5 GHz (cycle-scaled) | Practical floor | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\mathrm{Riem}^3$ ($n=12$) | 438.5 µs | 18.6 KB | ≈ 150 µs | 280 ns | ≈ 530× |
| $\mathrm{Riem}^4$ ($n=16$) | 1638.6 µs | 25.1 KB | ≈ 560 µs | — | — |

The laptop was on battery with the `powersave` governor. Its measured L1 latency (2.7–3.3 ns for Raptor
Cove's 5-cycle L1) implies an effective 1.5–1.8 GHz, so measured times were scaled by $1.7/5$. The gap is
indicative only, but it is large enough that no plausible scaling closes it: xperm itself is fast, and the
cost is per-call overhead — rebuilding Schreier–Sims chains, allocations, registry lookups — in the
Julia wrapper.

## 11. What is and is not established

- **Rigorous:** the I/O floor (§3). **Model lower bounds for this algorithm:** §4–§8, conditional on the
  µop counts per primitive and the peak rates in §1.
- **Not modelled:**
  - Failed search branches (the model assumes perfect pruning).
  - Non-$S_k$ symmetry groups: block symmetries, groups acting on pairs.
  - Multi-term (Bianchi) reduction, which is a separate linear-algebra layer.
  - Certificate serialization.
  - SMT and E-cores.
- **Most uncertain parameters:**
  - The GPU SIMT efficiency $\eta$. The parallel study notes that realised GPU speed-ups for related
    irregular graph-canonical-form workloads have been ≤ 9×, and that others' reports are "too
    optimistic".
  - The sustained $\iota$ for refinement code.
  - The mispredict rate $p$.
- **What to benchmark first:**
  1. A branch-free bitset refinement kernel on cases A–D against this table.
  2. The fraction of real terms (bench_12, golden masters) where refinement alone yields a discrete
     partition, i.e. $N_{\mathrm{ind}} = 0$.
  3. Batched versus naive hash insertion at $10^7$ distinct terms.
